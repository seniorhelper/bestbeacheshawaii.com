Know Before You Go

Beach Rules, Access & Reservations

Hawaiʻi's beaches belong to everyone, but a handful of popular parks now require advance booking, and protected wildlife comes with real legal limits. This page gathers the rules that most often surprise visitors.

Best Beaches Hawaii · Updated October 2026 · How we research
Public Access

The shoreline belongs to everyone

You will sometimes hear that "all beaches in Hawaiʻi are public." The more precise version is that the shoreline is public, and the state protects your right to walk along it.

Under Hawaiʻi Revised Statutes Chapter 115, beach transit corridors seaward of the shoreline are public property, and the Department of Land and Natural Resources says coastal landowners must keep vegetation from blocking that public right of passage. Intentionally obstructing someone's use of a beach transit corridor is treated as obstructing access to public property, a misdemeanor. The counties have primary responsibility for developing and maintaining public access paths from roads to the shore.

What this means in practice:

  • You may use the sand in front of a hotel or home, but you need a legal way to get there. Look for posted public shoreline access signs and designated public parking.
  • Resort facilities are not public. Loungers, cabanas, pools and restrooms belong to the property.
  • Respect private land and gated communities on the way to the beach, and do not park where signs forbid it.
Book Ahead

Beaches and parks that need a reservation

To manage crowds and protect fragile places, a few of Hawaiʻi's most visited spots limit daily entry for non-residents. Residents with Hawaiʻi ID are usually exempt or have separate rules.

Hanauma Bay Nature Preserve

Oʻahu · City and County of Honolulu

Non-residents book a timed entry online and pay an entry fee. The preserve is regularly closed on some weekdays and has had extended closures for infrastructure repairs, including in August 2026, with another closure expected in early 2027. Visitors watch a short orientation before going down to the beach.

Hāʻena State Park (Kēʻē Beach)

Kauaʻi · DLNR State Parks

Non-residents need an advance reservation for entry and, if driving, for parking. The park sits at the end of the North Shore road and includes Kēʻē Beach and the start of the Kalalau Trail. A shuttle option has been offered as an alternative to parking.

Waiʻānapanapa State Park

Maui · DLNR State Parks

Since April 2021, non-resident visitors have needed both an entry and a parking reservation for this black-sand beach on the Road to Hāna. Reservations cannot be bought on the day at the park, so book before you set out.

Entry fees, parking fees and booking windows change. Always confirm on the official City and County of Honolulu or DLNR State Parks websites linked under Sources rather than relying on a booking site or an older article.

Wildlife On The Sand

Monk seals and turtles: give them room

It is common to find a Hawaiian monk seal asleep on a beach, or a green sea turtle resting at the water's edge. Both are protected by federal law, and harassing a monk seal is also a Class C felony under Hawaiʻi state law. Lifeguards and volunteers often rope off a resting seal; stay outside the line.

Everyday Etiquette

Small habits locals appreciate

  • Pack out everything you bring, including cigarette butts and food scraps that attract pests.
  • Leave sand, shells, coral and lava rock where you find them. Removing natural materials is restricted in many protected areas.
  • Use mineral sunscreen or a rash guard; see our reef-safe sunscreen guide for the state and county rules.
  • Do not stand on coral or move rocks in tide pools; both are homes for marine life.
  • Respect cultural sites such as heiau and fishponds. If a sign asks you to stay out, stay out.
  • Share the lineup and the shore. Beach parks are local backyards, especially on weekends and holidays.
  • Lock nothing valuable in the car. Break-ins at beach parking lots are a known problem; travel light.
Common Questions

Beach Rules & Access, answered

Are all beaches in Hawaii public?

The shoreline itself is public. Under Hawaii law, beach transit corridors seaward of the shoreline are public property, and intentionally blocking someone from using them can be a misdemeanor. Access to the beach across private land is a different matter: use marked public access paths and parking, and do not cut through hotels or homes unless the path is designated.

Which Hawaii beaches need a reservation?

The best-known examples are Hanauma Bay Nature Preserve on Oʻahu, which uses timed reservations for non-residents; Hāʻena State Park on Kauaʻi, which includes Kēʻē Beach and requires non-resident entry and parking reservations; and Waiʻānapanapa State Park on Maui, where non-residents need entry and parking reservations booked in advance. Rules and fees change, so check the official sites.

How far should I stay from monk seals and turtles on the beach?

NOAA recommends at least 50 feet from Hawaiian monk seals, 150 feet from a mother with a pup, and at least 10 feet from sea turtles. Harassing a monk seal is a felony under Hawaii state law, and both species are protected by federal law.

Can I take sand, shells or lava rocks home from a Hawaii beach?

Please leave them. Removing natural materials harms beaches and the creatures that live there, and taking sand, rock or marine life is restricted in many protected areas such as Marine Life Conservation Districts and state parks.

Is it illegal to use certain sunscreens in Hawaii?

Hawaii bans the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone or octinoxate statewide. Maui County goes further and allows only mineral sunscreens, and Hawaiʻi County bans the sale of non-mineral sunscreens. A mineral sunscreen with zinc oxide or titanium dioxide works everywhere.
