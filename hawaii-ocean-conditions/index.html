Reading Hawaiʻi's Ocean Conditions

The right beach on the wrong day can be dangerous, and the wrong beach on the right day can be perfect. Learn the seasonal pattern, the official sources locals check, and the clues you can read from the sand.

Best Beaches Hawaii · Updated October 2026 · How we research
The Big Picture

Swell has a season and a direction

Every island has beaches facing north, south, east and west, and each one reacts differently to the same ocean. Once you know which way a beach faces, you can usually guess whether it will be calm.

Hawaiʻi's surf comes from storms far away. Between about October and April, deep winter storms near Alaska and the Aleutians push waves thousands of miles toward the islands, and they land with the most force on north- and west-facing sand. From roughly May to September, the swell source shifts to winter storms south of the equator, so it is the south-facing beaches that see surf. On top of that, the northeast trades that dominate Hawaiʻi's weather roughen up the windward side on most afternoons.

By Season

Which coast to choose, month by month

Winter roughly Oct–Apr

  • North & west shores: big-wave season. Famous for watching surfers, not for swimming.
  • South & leeward shores: usually the calmest choice.
  • Windward shores: often breezy with chop and passing showers.

Summer roughly May–Sep

  • North shores: often flatten out, opening up swimming and snorkeling.
  • South shores: south swells can bring surf and shorebreak.
  • Leeward coasts: sunny and dry, with afternoon wind in places.

Treat this as a rule of thumb. A single unseasonal swell can transform a normally gentle beach overnight, so check the forecast for the day you go, not just the month.

Official Sources

Where locals check before they go

Surf forecast & advisories

The National Weather Service in Honolulu posts expected wave heights for each coast. When it puts out an advisory or warning for high surf, choose a beach on a different side of the island.

Lifeguard-reported hazards

Lifeguards feed hazard reports into hawaiibeachsafety.com, a statewide map of guarded beaches showing flags for surf, currents, jellyfish and more.

Water quality

Brown-water and sewage-spill notices for the coast come from the state health department's Clean Water Branch.

Tides

NOAA Tides and Currents gives tide times. Low tide makes shallow reefs harder to cross; some tide pools and ledges are only safe to visit at low tide on calm days.

Read The Beach

Clues you can see from the sand

  • Count a few sets. Waves arrive in groups. Wait through at least two or three sets before judging the size.
  • Listen. A heavy, booming sound from waves hitting the sand usually means dangerous shorebreak.
  • Look at the sand line. A steep drop right at the water's edge means waves will break hard and close to shore.
  • Watch for gaps. A calm-looking channel between breaking waves can be a rip current carrying water out.
  • Check the rocks. If waves are washing over ledges and tide pools, stay off them.
  • See what the lifeguard flags say, and watch where experienced locals are, and are not, swimming.
Other Advisories

Rain, jellyfish and sirens

After heavy rain

Big rainstorms flush mud, debris and bacteria down streams and storm drains and into the shallows, and the water near a stream mouth can stay unhealthy well after the sky clears. If the ocean looks brown or murky, skip it; when health officials have posted an advisory, recent reports quoted guidance to give it two to three days after the rain stops. Rain also raises the risk of leptospirosis in rivers, streams and ponds, so keep children and anyone with open cuts out of fresh water after storms.

Jellyfish

Box jellyfish have a habit of drifting into south- and west-facing shallows roughly a week and a half after each full moon. When they show up, lifeguards put up warning signs, and the Waikīkī Aquarium posts a forecast calendar of likely days. On the windward side, onshore breezes sometimes push blue Portuguese man-of-war onto the sand; even stranded ones can sting. If you are stung, leave the water and ask a lifeguard for help.

Tsunami sirens

On the first business day of every month, just before noon (11:45 a.m.), you will hear the statewide sirens sound for around a minute as a routine test. Sirens on any other day are not a test: get off the beach, head inland or uphill, and follow instructions from the Hawaii Emergency Management Agency and local news.

Common Questions

Ocean Conditions, answered

Which side of the Hawaiian islands is calmest in winter?

During the winter months, roughly October to April, the biggest waves usually reach beaches facing north and west. Beaches facing south, and sheltered leeward coves, are generally the better bet for swimming then, though a swell from the south can still arrive.

Is summer a good time for North Shore beaches?

Usually. From about May to September the north shores of the islands often lie down, and beaches that are off-limits in winter can become calm enough to swim and snorkel. That is also when south-facing beaches are more likely to see waves.

What is a high surf advisory?

It is a National Weather Service alert that waves along a particular coast will be big enough to put beachgoers in danger. A high surf warning signals something more severe. On that coast, stay out of the ocean and keep well back from the water's edge and wave-washed rocks.

Should I swim after heavy rain in Hawaii?

Better not. Runoff after big storms can carry bacteria and debris into the shallows. Skip murky or brown water, especially near stream mouths, and follow any Department of Health advisory; recent reports have quoted officials suggesting a wait of two to three days after the rain ends.

Why do sirens sound in Hawaii at midday?

The statewide outdoor sirens get a routine test at 11:45 a.m. on the first business day of each month, lasting around a minute. If they sound at any other time, leave the beach and follow official emergency instructions.
